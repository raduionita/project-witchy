The 4 Phases of Your Menstrual Cycle, Explained as Seasons

What are the four phases of the menstrual cycle?

The menstrual cycle has four phases: menstrual, follicular, ovulatory and luteal. Together they make up the time from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. The average cycle is 28 days, but cycles from about 21 to 35 days are considered normal.

Winter: the menstrual phase (the Seed)

This is when you bleed. The uterus sheds its lining, and hormone levels are at their lowest. Periods usually last about three to seven days. Winter is a good time for rest, quiet and warmth. The gentle idea: keep things simple and let your body set the pace.

Spring: the follicular phase (the Sprout)

The follicular phase starts on the first day of your period and ends at ovulation. It is usually the longest phase, lasting around 14 to 21 days. Rising estrogen helps a dominant follicle mature into an egg. Many people feel more energy and motivation as this phase goes on. The gentle idea: try something new or start a project.

Summer: the ovulatory phase (the Bloom)

Ovulation happens when a surge in luteinizing hormone (LH) triggers the ovary to release a mature egg, roughly around day 14 of a 28-day cycle. The egg survives about 12 to 24 hours. Some people notice mild pelvic twinges, cervical mucus that is clear and stretchy, or a slight rise in mood or social energy. The gentle idea: connect with people and notice what feels good.

Autumn: the luteal phase (the Moth)

After ovulation, the body produces progesterone, and the luteal phase runs until your next period. It usually lasts about 12 to 14 days, with a normal range of about 10 to 17 days. Some people notice mood changes, tender breasts, bloating or appetite shifts in the days before a period. The gentle idea: slow down, wind down and make space for comfort.

Phase length varies

Only the luteal phase is fairly consistent from cycle to cycle. The follicular phase often changes length, which is why cycles differ from person to person and month to month. If your pattern is very different from the textbook version, that is common. Talk to a doctor if your cycle changes a lot or you have concerns.
